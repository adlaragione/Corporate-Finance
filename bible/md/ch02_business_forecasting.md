# PART A — From Equity Story to Value Drivers

## 2.4 Overview I — the five phases of business planning
1. Thorough analysis of company features, market(s), positioning and past performance
2. Definition of precise competitive strategies
3. Definition of actions needed to pursue these strategies
4. Definition of quantitative assumptions underpinning forecast financial statements
5. Preparation of forecast financial statements

## 2.5 Overview II — numbers without narrative
Three dangers when numbers are used without any narrative: **Plug-&-point exercise → Spreadsheet Nirvana → No logical tie breaker.**

In a good valuation numbers are bound together coherently and story-telling is kept grounded with numbers:
- develop a narrative for the business;
- test the narrative against history, experience and common sense;
- convert key parts of the narrative into value drivers;
- connect value drivers to a valuation;
- keep the feedback loop open.

**Key message: a business plan must be both narratively credible and financially coherent.**

(Added) "Plug-&-point": you plug numbers and point to the output; "spreadsheet nirvana": the model looks precise because it is detailed; "no logical tie breaker": when two analysts disagree, without a story there is no way to decide which number is more reasonable.

## 2.6 What is a coherent plan? (CORE)
A plan is coherent when:
1. the business story explains the value drivers;
2. value drivers map into financial-statement items;
3. sales growth is supported by capacity, CapEx and working capital;
4. margin improvement has an operational explanation;
5. financing assumptions are consistent with cash-flow deficits or surpluses;
6. long-run assumptions are reachable from the explicit forecast period.

**Bottom line: a plan can be plausible line by line and still be incoherent as a system.**

## 2.8 Market analysis (PRO)
To forecast accurately, analyse the markets the firm operates in. Complexity depends on:
- **Unrelated businesses** — the firm operates in unrelated businesses (holding firms);
- **Different geographical markets** — multinational firms;
- **Different distribution channels** — business units target different channels (e.g., proprietary website + network of retail stores);
- **Residual** — hybrid situations mixing the above.

## 2.9 Competitive strategy (PRO)
Prepared both at (1) the overall company level and (2) any single business-unit level.

| Company level | Business-unit level |
|---|---|
| Operating sectors | Geographical markets |
| Legal structure | Competitive positioning |
| Corporate structure | Distribution channel(s) |
| Finance structure | Business model |
| Tax planning | Supply chain integration |
| Intangibles | Physical places to occupy |

Example (screenshot): stationery/luxury-goods firm — **Distribution** (increase proximity to customers): Wholesale (direct model in key markets; trade marketing and space management; moderate doors increase), Retail (Retail 2.0; network development), eCommerce (increase conversion; develop omnichannel; boost traffic), B2B (large deals). **Product portfolio** (develop paper and non-paper categories): Paper (limited editions, premium, personalized), Bags & SLG (reinforce capabilities, specialized channel), [redacted] (franchising, JVs), Digital (new products/services with partners), Writing tools (tactical partnership).

## 2.10 Action-taking (CORE)
Once strategies are defined, focus on the actions needed to implement them. Each action should be matched with:
1. **Clear objectives** to be pursued;
2. **Impacts on financial statements**;
3. **Expected realization timing**;
4. **Execution risks and people in charge**.

The credibility of a business plan largely depends on how clearly the mapping of actions is set out.

Example (screenshot): Moderate doors increase (expand in APAC, new US channels e.g. Walmart, to balance decline of bookstores); Direct model in key markets (direct control of high-potential retailers in UK, France, Japan for higher profitability); Trade marketing and space management (visual merchandising in top doors, in-store activities, better space).

## 2.11 Quantitative assumptions (CORE)
Planning ends with a forecast financial statement, based on quantitative assumptions:
- **Macroeconomic** — different for each geographic area (GDP, inflation, forex…);
- **Business activity** — evolution of key financials (sales, costs, working capital, ratios…);
- **Corporate structure** — changes at corporate level (capital structure, fiscal policy, surplus assets…).

## 2.12 Revenue forecast assumptions (CORE)
Revenue tree: **Revenue = Price × Volume**; Volume ← **Market size** (growth, threats, opportunities) and **Market share** (uniqueness, awareness, loyalty, patents); share levers: advertising, promotion, sales force, distribution coverage, availability.

> Revenue = Price × Volume = Price × Market size × Market share

**Professional rule: do not forecast revenue growth as one number unless you can explain the whole logic behind it.**

(Added) Growth decomposition: (1 + g_Rev) = (1 + g_price)(1 + g_market)(1 + g_share). E.g., price +2%, market +5%, share +3% → 1.02 × 1.05 × 1.03 − 1 = 10.3%.

## 2.13 Modelling (CORE)
Market analysis + Strategy + Hypotheses & Actions → funnel → **Integrated Forecast Financial Statement** ("this is where the equity story becomes numbers").

# PART B — Building the Integrated Forecast Model

## 2.14 Modelling basics I–II: the 10 steps (CORE)
1. Set up the model template
2. Input historical financials
3. Calculate ratios
4. Build assumptions
5. Project the IS (**leave out interest**)
6. Project the BS (**leave out cash and debt**)
7. Project the CF statement
8. Balance the BS
9. Model interest expense/income
10. Create summary/output pages

(Added) Why leave out interest, cash and debt: they depend on the financing need, which is only known once operations are projected. Financing is the last layer; it must not "plug" operating mistakes.

## 2.15 Main aspects of model set-up (PRO)
1. One sheet vs multi-sheet model.
2. **Matrix integrity** — a given column refers to a specific year in all sheets.
3. Key sheets: assumptions/inputs; secondary calculations; debt schedule; IS; BS; CF statement; summary ratios; output.

## 2.16 Main precautions (PRO)
1. Modelling standards: (a) **Accurate** — always proof-read (sanity, matrix and stress check); (b) **Coherent** — assumptions on individual items fit together; flexible — no hard numbers in formulae; user friendly — break down complex calculations.
2. Learn keyboard shortcuts.
3. Use comments to indicate sources.
4. Formatting and colour coding: inputs, links to other sheets and formulae in three different colours.
5. Be able to print the model at any time.

## 2.17 Modelling basics III — historical inputs (CORE)
1. Typically **3–5 years** of historical data.
2. IS and BS line items reflect the structure of the company's published financials.
3. Some items must be forecast separately even if not broken down: **D&A** (usually inside COGS and SG&A; total found in the CF statement); **interest expense** (can be net of interest income); **interest income** (netted or in other income); **non-recurring items** (identify and separate).
4. Some items may be aggregated (other assets/liabilities).

## 2.18 Modelling basics IV — ratios and assumptions (PRO)
1. Key historical ratios: IS (growth, margins); BS (days receivable, payable, inventory).
2. Assumptions on their evolution based on: historical trends; strategic assessment; market consensus for the company; consensus for peers.
3. Cross-check vs: historical trends (abrupt jumps?); **internal coherence** (related items evolve coherently?); **external consistency** (in line with consensus?).

## 2.19 Modelling basics V–VI — model architecture (TECH)
Blocks: **Income statement** (revenues, opex, EBIT, interest, EBT, taxes, NI); **Retained earnings** (beginning, + NI, − preferred dividends, − common dividends, ending); **Balance sheet** (operating assets, fixed assets; operating liabilities, debt, preferred, common, retained earnings); **Free cash flows** (EBIT − taxes on EBIT + non-cash expenses − Δ required cash − ΔWC − CapEx = unlevered FCF; − interest + interest tax shield − preferred dividends + Δdebt + Δpreferred = equity FCF; − common dividends = Δ excess cash; + Δ required cash = Δ cash); **Supporting schedules**: competitive analysis (economy, industry, comparables), fixed assets (capacity needs, fixed-asset requirements, CapEx, depreciation), income taxes (expense, deferred, NOL carryforward, payable), financing (debt and interest, preferred, common).

Slide VI (screenshot): arrows show how every block links; the financing items (interest, debt, preferred stock, interest tax shield, change in debt) are crossed out and the Financing schedule is crossed out — "Done!!". (Added) i.e., the **operating (unlevered) model can be completed without any financing assumption**: unlevered FCF does not depend on financing.

## 2.20 Projecting the IS (CORE/PRO)
- **Sales** (forecast growth): price growth drivers (inflation, strategy); volume growth drivers (total market vs market share).
- **COGS and SG&A**: as a % of sales.
- **Depreciation**: as a % of PPE at the **beginning** of the period.
- **Amortization**: based on specific intangible-asset estimation.
- **Interest income and expense**: leave out until the rest of the model is done.
- **Taxes**: consider effective vs marginal tax rate.

Coherence checks: if revenue grows, do margins, D&A and capacity still make sense? If debt, cash, taxes or interest change, do projected interest and tax follow?

Further issues (PRO): **Net income** — normalize for non-recurring items; **EPS** — basic vs diluted; net income vs normalized; **DPS** — DPS growth rate vs payout ratio; **Equity** — use NI and dividends to complete the equity base analysis.

## 2.21 Projecting the BS (CORE/PRO)
- **Working capital**: driven by sales and COGS (receivable, inventory and payable days).
- **Fixed assets** (PPE schedule in secondary calcs): CapEx as a fixed component + % of sales; maintenance vs expansion.
- **Goodwill**: keep it flat.
- **Financial investments**: fair value estimated to stay flat.
- **Other operating liabilities**: driven by sales.
- **Equity**: from the secondary calculation sheet.
- **Short and long-term debt**: leave out until the rest of the model is done.

Coherence checks: if revenue grows, do receivables, inventories, payables and PPE grow consistently? Are cash and debt balancing the model, or hiding operating-forecast mistakes?

(Added) Days formulas (on ending balances):
- Receivables = Revenue × DSO / 365
- Inventory = COGS × DIO / 365
- Payables = COGS × DPO / 365
- Net PPE = (Net PPE / Revenue) × Revenue; CapEx = ΔNet PPE + Depreciation.

## 2.22 Projecting the CF statement (CORE/PRO)
- **You are 100% certain to obtain the change in cash from the change in all non-cash BS accounts.**
- The CF statement captures the change in every BS item and yields the change in cash.
- Rules: **assets up → cash down; liabilities up → cash up**; ΔWC captures operating assets/liabilities; CapEx and D&A capture fixed assets; net income and dividends capture retained earnings.

1. **Net cash flow** = operating CF + investing CF + financing CF; **Beginning cash + Net cash flow = Ending cash**.
2. **Cash available for debt repayment (CADR)**: operating CF (from NI) + investing CF + equity issuance − dividends/repurchases = **CFADR**; − cash required (day-to-day) = **CADR**. **Beginning cash + CFADR − Cash required = CADR.**

## 2.23 Final procedures (TECH/PRO)
I. Balance the BS: complete the debt schedule; use a **cash sweep** — if CADR > 0 it repays debt (starting from short-term, then long-term); if CADR < 0, short-term debt is issued to cover the shortfall and the minimum operating cash; link short- and long-term debt balances to the BS (and CF statement); link the resulting cash balance to the BS.

II. **Circularity**: if interest expense is computed on average debt and interest income on average cash: interest → net income → cash flow → debt/cash ending balance → interest… The (less accurate) alternative without iteration: use **opening balances** of debt and cash.

III. Summary/output: ratios on projected performance (growth, activity/asset turnover, solvency/credit, profitability, returns); output sheets easy to paste into presentations and printable.

# PART C — JJ Jones Case

## 2.24 JJ Jones value drivers
Income statement: revenue growth 10%; COGS 60% of revenues; operating expenses 10% of revenues; depreciation 10 years; tax rate 40% on all income.
Assets: excess assets $0; required cash 5% of revenues; AR 50 days*; inventory 80 days*; other current assets 6% of revenues; net PPE/revenues 1.2*; other assets $0.
Liabilities and preferred: AP 40 days*; accrued expenses 20% of revenues; non-current liabilities 10% of revenues; other income $0; interest rate 8% (beginning balance); preferred dividend 10% (beginning balance); debt — retire current portion of LT debt, increase LT debt if insufficient cash flow (same rate); preferred stock no change.
(* based on ending balance)

Discussion questions: prepare (i) IS forecasts, (ii) PPE forecasts, (iii) BS forecasts, (iv) FCF forecasts.

What JJ Jones tests: (1) assumptions → statements; (2) three statements linked; (3) required vs excess cash; (4) WC from days ratios; (5) PPE from PPE/revenue; (6) debt/cash balance the model instead of plugging operations; (7) FCFO and FCFE from the resulting statements.

## 2.26 Common mistakes in business forecasting (slide)
1. Forecasting a hockey stick without explaining the operational mechanism.
2. Forecasting the most likely case instead of expected cash flows.
3. Growing sales while leaving CapEx or WC flat.
4. Improving margins without explaining pricing power, cost efficiency or mix.
5. Ignoring cyclicality and normalizing the wrong year.
6. Letting cash or debt become the hidden plug.
7. Treating the business plan as a spreadsheet exercise rather than a story-to-numbers exercise.

## 2.27 Takeaways
1. A business plan starts with a story but is only useful for valuation when the story becomes value drivers.
2. Forecasts must be integrated: IS, BS, CF, debt schedule and outputs must reconcile.
3. Decompose revenue growth into price, volume, market size and market share whenever possible.
4. CapEx and WC assumptions must be coherent with sales growth and capacity needs.
5. A good model does not hide problems in cash, debt or plug variables.
6. JJ Jones is the first full exercise in translating value drivers into forecast statements and cash flows.

## Formula Summary Sheet
- Revenue = Price × Market size × Market share; (1+g) = (1+g_p)(1+g_m)(1+g_s)
- AR = Rev × DSO/365; Inventory = COGS × DIO/365; AP = COGS × DPO/365
- Net PPE = (PPE/Rev) × Rev; CapEx = ΔNet PPE + Dep; Dep = Gross PPE_begin / useful life (or % of beginning PPE)
- Interest = rate × beginning debt (avoids circularity)
- Beginning cash + Net cash flow = Ending cash
- CFADR = CFO + CFI + equity issuance − dividends; CADR = beginning cash + CFADR − cash required
- Assets ↑ → cash ↓; liabilities ↑ → cash ↑
- FCFO = EBIT(1−t) + Dep − ΔWC − ΔRC − CapEx; FCFE = FCFO − Int(1−t) − Pref div + Net debt

## Common Mistakes & Exam Traps
1. Interest, cash and debt are projected LAST (IS without interest; BS without cash and debt).
2. Cash/debt must balance the model, not hide operating errors (no hidden plug).
3. Depreciation on **beginning** PPE; interest on **beginning** debt avoids circularity; average balances → circular model.
4. Days ratios: receivables on revenue; inventory on COGS; payables on COGS or, as in the official JJ Jones solution, on purchases (COGS + ΔInventory); JJ Jones uses ending balances.
5. Revenue growth must be decomposed (price × volume; market size × share).
6. Sales growth requires CapEx and WC growth (capacity).
7. Expected cash flows, not the most likely case.
8. Cash sweep repays short-term debt first, then long-term; negative CADR → issue short-term debt.
9. Goodwill and financial investments: kept flat.
10. Assets up = cash down; liabilities up = cash up.
11. A plan can be plausible line by line and incoherent as a system.
12. Each action needs objectives, financial impact, timing, execution risks and people in charge.
13. Required cash ≠ excess cash (JJ Jones: required cash 5% of revenue; excess assets 0).
14. The unlevered model (through unlevered FCF) needs no financing assumption.
